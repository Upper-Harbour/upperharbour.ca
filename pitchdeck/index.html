The problem

Canadian privacy compliance is broken at every price point.

Quebec Law 25, Alberta POPA, BC PIPA, federal PIPEDA, eight more provincial regimes — and the tools to comply with them all assume the buyer is either a Bay Street firm or a Silicon Valley startup. Neither describes the Canadian businesses actually on the hook.

01

Generic AI paraphrases the statute.

Ask ChatGPT or Claude to draft a PIA. The output reads plausibly but cites nothing. A regulator wants the words that appear in section 17 of the Act, not an LLM's summary of them. There's no audit trail, no provenance, no way to verify the citation against canonical source.

02

US tools don't understand Canadian regimes.

Termly, iubenda, OneTrust, TrustArc — all built for GDPR and CCPA. They don't know that Alberta POPA replaces FOIP in June 2026, that Law 25 s. 17 has specific transfer-impact assessment requirements, or that the Plixer doctrine means data residency isn't data sovereignty. A school board in Calgary needs Alberta-specific compliance, not a templated US privacy policy.

03

Consultants cost $20,000 per PIA.

For an SMB, a small public body, or a regulated mid-market organization, this isn't compliance — it's a tax that gets deferred until the regulator notices. The Athena Collective, our pilot, came to us with a $20,000 quote for a single Law 25 transfer impact assessment they couldn't justify spending.

04

Sovereignty is invisible in the existing tooling.

Vendors disclose where data is hosted; almost none disclose the jurisdiction of their ultimate parent corporation or the legal regime under which compelled disclosure could occur. Of the SaaS tools surveyed in our Sovereignty Index, the majority are CLOUD Act exposed without their Canadian customers knowing it.

The solution

A structured authority layer for Canadian privacy law — and the document infrastructure built on top of it.

Upper Harbour is not an AI tool. It is a structured Canadian privacy data graph, queryable through documents we generate and an API we expose to AI builders. The model is Claude. The intellectual property is two years of statute curation and 768 hand-classified SaaS vendors. The orchestration is what makes the output auditable instead of plausible.

Layer 1 · Authority

Verbatim Canadian statute corpus.

Every section of every Canadian privacy regime, extracted directly from canonical King's Printer PDFs, structured by jurisdiction → act → section, with provenance and in-force dates. Not training data. Not paraphrase. The actual statute text, audit-trail attached.

Layer 2 · Sovereignty Index

768 SaaS tools, three layers deep.

Each vendor classified against corporate parent, jurisdiction of incorporation, CLOUD Act exposure, data-residency posture, and subprocessor chain. Slack is "US" because Salesforce Inc. is its parent, not because the servers are in San Francisco. The Plixer doctrine, programmatically applied.

Layer 3 · Orchestration

Documents that cite the law verbatim.

When a customer says "we use Slack," our system knows Slack → Salesforce → US → CLOUD Act → Law 25 s. 17 applies → here are the verbatim words → here is the generated paragraph. Claude writes the prose; the graph supplies the facts. Every claim ties to canonical source through Anthropic's Citations API.

Why now

Three forces converged in the last 18 months.

01

Canadian privacy regulators have teeth for the first time.

Quebec Law 25 came fully into force in 2024 with maximum penalties of 4% of worldwide turnover. Alberta's Protection of Privacy Act (POPA) replaces FOIP for public bodies on June 11, 2026 — with the same level of enforcement infrastructure. BC PIPA, Ontario MFIPPA, federal PIPEDA reforms are queued behind. The era of treating Canadian privacy as a documentation exercise is ending; the era of treating it as enforceable law is beginning.

02

LLMs became technically capable of verbatim grounding.

The Anthropic Citations API and equivalent retrieval-augmented architectures crossed the threshold where "every claim ties to a span in the source document" is a real product feature, not a research demo. Before this, AI legal tools could only paraphrase. Now they can cite.

03

Sovereignty became a Canadian conversation.

CLOUD Act exposure, federal Buy Canadian framework, the public-sector procurement debates around US cloud providers, the rising visibility of data-residency-is-not-data-sovereignty as a structural problem — all of this is happening now, in Canada, in 2025-2026. We've been publishing on this for two years in Maclean's, Policy Options, and OpenCanada. The discourse has caught up.

Market and trajectory

Canadian privacy is the wedge. Canadian legal data infrastructure is the company.

The immediate market. Canadian SMBs and mid-market regulated organizations collectively spend an estimated $5-8 billion annually on legal and compliance services. Capturing 1% as a structured legal-data platform is a $50-80M ARR business — too small for Anthropic or Thomson Reuters to verticalize, but big enough for a focused company to dominate.

The expansion path. The graph architecture extends naturally beyond privacy. Employment law (provincial ESAs, AODA, ACA, accommodation records) is the obvious year-two expansion — same buyer, same Canadian-jurisdictional structure, doubles ARPU per customer. Corporate records (BCBCA, OBCA, ABCA, QBCA), securities (NI 45-106), health regulation (PHIPA, HIA) follow on the same architectural primitives.

Why this is hard to copy. The 768-tool Sovereignty Index and 13-regime authority corpus took two years to build. A competitor would need both Canadian privacy domain expertise and the time to extract, structure, and verify the data. Anthropic, OneTrust, and Thomson Reuters have the engineering capacity but lack the domain motivation; Canadian law firms have the domain expertise but no track record building structured-data products.

Why this compounds. The Sovereignty Index is shared infrastructure: every customer onboarding adds vendor classifications that every future customer inherits. The authority registry verifies under real use: each generated PIA exercises specific statute sections, surfacing gaps and edge cases the curated verification effort would take months to find independently. Most importantly, the value of every monitoring event — a vendor's M&A, a statutory amendment, a regulator's new guidance — scales with the number of customer documents in production. A Salesforce reorg flags 30 dependent docs at 10 customers, 300 at 100, 3,000 at 1,000. The data layer is a flywheel, not a network effect — but the flywheel is real, and it spins faster as use compounds.

Why now matters. The 12-24 month window where this category doesn't exist yet is closing. Either we build it or someone else does. The two years of published research, the working product, and the pilot customer already on the record are a meaningful head start.
